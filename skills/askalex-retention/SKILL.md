# AskAlex Retention

## Start with the economics

Before any tactic, be clear about why this is worth doing at all:

**It costs 5 to 25 times more to acquire a customer than to keep one.** Churn is not a customer-service problem; it is a margin problem wearing a friendly face.

The arithmetic that makes the case: reducing churn by 3% is *equivalent* to increasing acquisition by 3.3x. Which of those two is easier?

And the compounding is nasty in the other direction — if you lose 10% of customers a month but only gain 10% through referrals, you stay flat forever. You have to beat churn or increase referrals just to stand still.

Retention also makes everything upstream cheaper: positive word of mouth, better talent, easier fundraising. But the core argument is the 5-25x.

## The one thing that matters most: the activation point

**All roads lead to activation.** Finding your activation point has a greater effect on churn than anything else on this list. Make it the top priority.

An activation point is the *specific behaviour, at a specific frequency, that correlates with a customer staying a long time.* It is a number, and it is different for every business:

| Business | Activation point |
|---|---|
| Fitness studio | 5 workouts in the first month |
| B2B service | the first time they get leads |
| Software | the first time they log in and see the dashboard |
| Community platform | reaching ~3 members |
| Physical product | first repeat use |

**How to find yours — reverse-engineer the survivors:**

1. **Pull your churned customers.** Gather everything about them.
2. **Pull your best customers** — the top 20% who stayed longest and spent most.
3. **Compare.** How did the two groups use the thing, and how did you treat each?
4. **Make the best ones the template.** Then update your *messaging* to attract people like them, and your *onboarding* to push everyone through the activation point fast.

That last step is the highest-leverage one in retention: Alex's gym business went from 8% to 3% monthly churn within six months essentially by doing this, and the same move produced 70x more profit than a competitor selling to the same volume of people — because he pre-qualified for the profile that stays.

## The decay pattern — and when to intervene

Churn is almost never sudden. There is a visible slope before the cancellation, and it looks like this:

```
Week 1: 3 sessions
Week 2: 2 sessions   ← reach out here
Week 3: 2 sessions
Week 4: 1 session
Week 5: CANCEL
```

**Usage churn is the leading indicator.** A customer who is still subscribed but has stopped using the thing has already left; the billing just hasn't caught up. Track usage, not just cancellations.

The move: catch them at the *first* drop, not at the cancellation. Praise participation and progress, and solve the small problem that's in their way. Do both.

## Why people actually leave

**The #1 reason for churn is overwhelm.** Not price, not a competitor — overwhelm. Counterintuitive but consistent: retention comes down to making sure customers **consume** the value, not bombarding them with it.

This is why "adding more to the product" often makes retention worse. Decide the core two or three things you deliver and make those excellent. There is wisdom in deletion.

**Two lists worth memorising.** What destroys trust:

- break promises
- miscommunicate
- treat them poorly
- set unrealistic expectations
- hide progress and other updates

And its mirror image, what builds it:

- communicate clearly
- treat them like royalty
- set realistic expectations
- give status updates
- connect them with other happy customers

**Price, value, and churn are one system.** Price is what they pay, value is what they get. Keep value above price and they stay. You can do that by delivering more value or charging less — and *raising price while value stays flat is a churn event waiting to happen.*

## The mechanics

Ordered roughly by how reliably they work:

- **Onboarding.** Personal outperforms group. Live outperforms recorded. Carrots outperform sticks. And *some beats none* — a basic onboarding today beats a perfect one next quarter. Use it to establish the long-term communication rhythm: customers should always know when they'll hear from you next.
- **Reach out proactively**, twice a week early on, praising progress and removing small obstacles.
- **Handwritten cards** at signup and at milestones. Low tech, disproportionately effective.
- **Cancellation call or video.** Tell customers at signup that you require one. Then actually get them on the phone: let them vent, get *more* upset than they are, confirm what would make them happy, and resell them on why they started. **Done right this saves about half of all phone and email cancellations.**
- **Exit interviews** for everyone who leaves. The data is the point.
- **Commitment structures.** Annual billing; or "buy 10 get 2 free" when average lifespan exceeds 12 months. Forcing commitment decreases sales *and* decreases churn — sometimes the trade is worth it, sometimes not. Test.
- **Community.** The principle: it's easy to quit a membership, it's hard to leave a relationship. Manually connect customers to each other and host group events. Do the intros yourself.
- **Unlockables and status.** Time-based unlocks placed just past average lifespan, then 30 days later, then 3 months, then 3 months. Courses that unlock, 1-1 calls at milestones, event tickets. Make staying feel like progress.
- **A customer journey** with four milestones: **activate → testimonial → refer → ascend.** Incentivize each. The last one matters — customers get an itch to buy more over time, and they'll buy it from you or from someone else.
- **Fire or correct bad customers.** Some churn is healthy; keeping the wrong customers poisons the experience for the right ones.

## A warning about measurement

If you implement retention work properly, **churn often goes UP in month one before it comes down.** In the gym rollout it rose 50% in month one, then fell 50% in month two and another 50% in month three.

This is expected, and it's called "shaking the three": some of those people were going to cancel anyway, and some had already churned as consumers while automated billing kept running — your outreach simply reminded them to cancel. Do not abandon a working retention program because month one looks worse.

## How this differs from askalex-ltv

Retention and LTV are adjacent and easy to confuse.

**This skill is about why the relationship ends.** It is diagnostic and behavioural — activation points, onboarding, expectations, saves.

**askalex-ltv is about how much the relationship is worth.** It is arithmetic — gross profit × lifespan, and the eight levers.

Churn is literally one input into the LTV formula, so the two stack: fixing retention extends the lifespan, and LTV work then extracts more from each month. **But if customers are leaving unhappy, LTV levers make it worse.** Fix the leak before turning up the pressure.

## Output shape

**1. The number** — current churn (monthly, and implied lifetime), and whether it is improving or degrading. If they don't track usage separately from cancellation, say so; that's the first gap.

**2. Where in the lifecycle** — month 3 cliff? First-week drop-off? Gradual decay? Each has a different cause and the answer changes completely. A cliff at month 3 means something promised at sale stops being delivered around then.

**3. The activation hypothesis** — what behaviour you believe predicts staying, and how to confirm it from their data this week.

**4. Three interventions** — from the mechanics above, with the leading indicator that would tell you each one is working.
